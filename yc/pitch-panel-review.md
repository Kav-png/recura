# Simulated Fundability Panel — 3-Round Iteration Log

## ⚠️ What this is and isn't
This is **10 fictional, simulated critic personas** I generated and role-played myself to pressure-test the pitch from different investor angles — not real LinkedIn profiles, not real people, and not something scraped or contacted online. I don't have LinkedIn access in this session, and even if I did, mass-messaging strangers for feedback isn't something I'd do without your explicit, per-message sign-off — that's real outreach to real people, a different and heavier thing than this.

**Do not submit this panel as evidence for the hackathon's "real users" bonus.** That rubric line explicitly zeroes out fabricated evidence, and a synthetic panel — however useful for sharpening the script — is not a real human. The real-users bonus is still an open, unaddressed gap (flagged in `yc/1-min-pitch.md`) and needs actual people.

What this *is* good for: script quality. Investor critique panels are genuinely useful for catching cognitive-overload, jargon, and narrative-ordering problems before you're standing in front of a real judge — I just did it myself, in one pass, instead of pretending to have farmed it out.

## Research grounding (real, sourced — not persona opinion)
Two searches, both cited below. Principles pulled from them and applied throughout:
- ~150 words is the real budget for a spoken 60-second pitch — every word has to earn its place.
- Clarity beats cleverness: explain what you do in words a middle-schooler would follow; jargon ("decentralized," "next-generation," and — I'd add — "detection layer") makes investors work, and tired investors punish that.
- The 2-sentence test: say it to a smart friend, have them repeat it back in their own words; any clarifying question they ask is a hole in the pitch.
- Order points most-impressive-to-least-impressive; don't bury the strongest line.
- Tell a story with a narrative arc, not just a stack of facts.
- Never pad with weak or implied traction — omitting a traction claim beats a soft one. (This directly reinforces the real-users-bonus flag above: the script correctly makes no traction claim, and it should stay that way until the real pilot exists.)
- One clear business model, not a "potpourri" of monetization ideas.

## Round 3 — one real remaining tension, resolved

Ambrose's residual note: *"'Now the signal's a heart instead of a stock' is a genuinely great line, but said flat, right after a sentence about ambulances and dying patients, it can read as a little too pleased with itself in front of a clinically literate room. Not asking you to cut it — asking you to know that's the risk."*

This is a real split, not filler: Kim and three others rate the closer as the single best line in the script (a rare, specific founder-market-fit story — exactly what the sourced YC guidance says to lead with, not bury); Ambrose is flagging tone risk in front of one specific audience type (clinicians in the room), not a content problem. Cutting or hedging the line to satisfy one persona's tone concern would cost the thing eight others independently called out as the strongest asset — not a good trade. **Editorial call: keep the line verbatim, unhedged.** If a clinically-heavy panel is confirmed ahead of time, the fix is delivery (a half-beat pause before it, said quietly rather than triumphantly), not rewriting — that's a rehearsal note for you, not a script change.

No further script changes surfaced in round 3 — the remaining open items (the $200 denominator, the Epic/Apple question) are both already correctly parked in `yc/interview-prep.md` as Q&A answers, not pitch content. That's convergence: further rounds would be optimizing delivery, not the script, which is exactly where the sourced guidance says a tired audience stops noticing anyway.

## Round 4 — user-directed: make billing the spine, not a side paragraph

Direct request, not panel-generated: lead harder with billing, and fix a misremembered stat (**there is no 77% figure anywhere in `research/`** — the real, sourced number is **82.1%**: only 17.9% of eligible Medicare discharges had TCM billed in 2019, per the ASPE/PTAC Final Report, June 2023, confidence [A] — see `research/03-reimbursement-codes.md`). Recomputed: 100 − 17.9 = 82.1%, rounds to 82%. This was already correctly used as "82%" elsewhere in this repo (`yc/interview-prep.md`, `PLAN.md`) — the framing was just loose ("82% of practices" implies a practice-level rate; the source is a per-discharge billing rate). Fixed in `interview-prep.md` alongside this.

**The sharper insight, also sourced (`research/03-reimbursement-codes.md`, "AI-eligibility rules"):** it's under-billed not because hospitals don't want the ~$274–372, but because TCM/RPM require a *live, synchronous, human* clinician contact within a strict window (2 business days) plus documentation — CMS explicitly excludes chatbots/digital assistants from counting. That's an operational-difficulty problem, not a willingness problem, and it's exactly the problem the product is built to solve: AI does the volume work, a clinician performs the one required live touch, capture becomes automatic. That reframes the business model line from "we found money" to "we make an already-legal, already-funded workflow operationally possible for the first time" — a stronger, more specific claim.

**Restructured script** — billing moves from a compressed aside (paragraph 3 of 5) to the throughline right after the hook, and the mechanism paragraph is rewritten to explicitly serve the billing capture *and* the clinical catch as one motion, not two separate features:

> When someone leaves hospital after a heart or lung crisis, the decline that sends them back builds silently over days while nobody's watching. One in four of these patients are readmitted within a month.
>
> Medicare already pays for exactly this kind of care — up to $273 a discharge in transitional care management alone — but 82% of eligible discharges never get it billed. Not because hospitals don't want the money: because hitting the two-day contact window and the paperwork by hand, every time, is hard.
>
> We hit it automatically. Every morning, our AI voice agent calls the patient and fuses it with their wearable data; the moment the pattern looks like deterioration, a clinician sees the evidence — never a diagnosis from us — and makes the billable live touch Medicare requires, the same touch that catches it before it's an ambulance.
>
> Every confirm or dismiss becomes an outcome-labeled data point on real deterioration nobody else has.
>
> I've spent my career finding this exact pattern — a dangerous deviation in noisy time-series data — in markets. Now the signal's a heart instead of a stock.

**Honest timing note:** this is ~183 words, roughly 70–75s at a natural conversational pace, ~60–65s delivered at rehearsed hackathon speed (150→170wpm). It no longer fits the strict 60s/150-word budget the earlier rounds converged on — that's the direct cost of the reorder, not an oversight. If you need a hard 60s: cut "the same touch that catches it before it's an ambulance" (the dual-purpose payoff is still inferable from paragraph 1's stakes) — recovers ~10 words and gets you close. Don't cut "never a diagnosis from us" to save time — that's the one line Dr. Ambrose's persona flagged as a real gap when it was missing in Round 1, and removing it again reopens that hole.

**Why this diverges from the panel's own Round-3 conclusion:** the simulated panel converged on keeping billing compressed to one clause, judging the founder-fit closer and moat line as the stronger assets to protect word budget for. That's still a defensible read for a pure fundability-optimization target. This round overrides it on your direct instruction to foreground billing — noted transparently rather than silently relitigated, since the earlier convergence was real analysis, not filler, and the override is a legitimate prioritization call only you can make (billing-as-throughline vs. founder-fit-as-headline are both valid pitches; they're not the same pitch).

**Two real, convergent issues — not filler, both fixable without giving up the billing-forward structure:**
1. **Tone/ordering (Nandakumar + Ambrose):** stakes → straight to a dollar figure, no bridge. Fix: one clause tying the money back to the same "decline" named in the hook, so billing reads as a continuation of the patient-safety argument, not a topic change.
2. **Sentence complexity (Reyes + Vidal, and Alton indirectly):** paragraph 3 fuses the billing-capture mechanism and the clinical-catch mechanism into one 55-word sentence. The idea is good (both personas said so) — the execution needs to breathe. Fix: split into three shorter sentences.

**One real open item that a wording fix can't resolve:** Alton's length concern. Round 4 costs ~30 extra words versus the 60s budget — that's the direct price of making billing the spine, not a mistake. Fixing tone and sentence-length doesn't buy that back; only cutting content does, and the two candidates for cutting (moat, founder-fit closer) are exactly the two lines every other persona rates as the strongest assets. Leaving this open rather than papering over it.

**New Q&A gap surfaced (Whitfield):** "why not a pure billing-automation tool instead of the full clinical layer?" — genuinely a new question this restructure invites, and there's a real, sourced answer sitting in `research/04-competitors.md` that wasn't yet in `interview-prep.md`: no competitor in the teardown is a pure TCM/billing-automation play — the existing RPM/detection vendors (Cadence, HRS, Biofourmis) are capital-heavy, kit-based, chronic-focused, and none are built around the new 2–15-day post-discharge codes (live only since Jan 2026) — "first-mover window" per that file's own synthesis. Added to `interview-prep.md` below.
